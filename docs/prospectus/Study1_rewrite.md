**Study 1**

**Background**

Cognitive models are used to represent psychological mechanisms. These models provide a powerful way to precisely and mathematically formalize how cognitive processes, such as ambiguity attitudes, influence people's decisions and behaviors. One threat to the internal validity of a computational model is whether the psychological parameters carry the same meaning across different groups of people. For example, one person's decision to steal may be driven by reduced sensitivity to uncertainty, while another person's may be largely influenced by limited socioeconomic resources that motivate them to steal. This is known as measurement noninvariance and can lead to biased parameter estimates, worse model fit, and items that fail to reflect the underlying construct of interest. In computational modeling, the influence of background factors on psychological processes is not fully understood. Specifically, it is unknown how omitting contextual factors influences model comparison and selection, a common practice in computational modeling.

Consider what a researcher observes when a contextual factor shapes a psychological parameter but is left out of the model. Among people who look alike on the variables the researcher recorded, the omitted context still varies, so the underlying preference still varies. The observed choices are therefore a blend of choice patterns from many people whose preferences were pushed in different directions by circumstances the model never represents. Blending choices this way can create patterns a restricted context-omitting model cannot reproduce, such as curvature in the choice function or dependence between a participant’s responses. A model with sufficient shared random-effect structure may already capture these patterns. Context disappears from the model, but not from the data being fit.

Model comparison rewards whichever candidate reproduces the observed responses most closely. A more complex model is more flexible, and that flexibility can be spent absorbing the leftover structure that omitted context created. When it is, standard selection tools point toward the wrong cognitive mechanism. Decision-making under ambiguity is a natural case, because people from different socioeconomic backgrounds are exposed to varying levels of environmental uncertainty that can shape their attitudes and decisions under ambiguity. Preferences for ambiguity can be captured in a single parameter, often called ambiguity aversion, whereas more complex models, such as the smooth model of ambiguity, separate a person's beliefs about how likely each outcome is from their attitude toward the ambiguity itself. While these richer models can plausibly explain the data better than simpler ones, failing to account for context can lead researchers to choose models with more cognitive parameters rather than models that appropriately situate people's decisions in their corresponding context.

In Study 1, we will establish the conditions under which model misidentification occurs, and the conditions under which it does not. Next, we will verify these conditions through simulations calibrated to the parameter ranges and sample sizes typical of published decision-making studies, estimating when false-complex selection occurs and how its frequency changes with participant count and task length. These simulations will test the prediction rather than assume high rates or monotonic worsening with more trials.

**Methods**

First, we characterize the participant response distribution obtained by integrating the product of trial choice probabilities over shared omitted context. Our constructive proof compares a linear utility-sensitivity model with a model that adds quadratic utility curvature. Both candidates omit context and both are false as joint response distributions, but the quadratic candidate has strictly smaller observable KL risk in the specified two-trial example. We then derive separate BIC, Bayes-factor and exact leave-one-participant-out corollaries under explicit sampling, parameter-space and prior conditions. BIC has an exact threshold in the realized likelihood gain; Bayes factors integrate likelihood over priors, and leave-one-participant-out evaluates the posterior predictive probability of a new participant’s entire response vector. We do not posit one universal finite sample at which all criteria must agree. Simulations will estimate selection probabilities and uncertainty. We will assess extension to structural ambiguity-aversion models separately and report cases where the simpler model already represents the omitted-context distribution.

We will then conduct a simulation study, generating data from a one-parameter ambiguity model in which a contextual factor shifts the ambiguity parameter. Although we will never generate data from a more complex two-parameter model, we will test whether it nonetheless fits better when both models omit context. We will cross five sample sizes (40, 100, 250, 500, and 1,000 participants), three task lengths (40, 100, and 200 trials), two socioeconomic distributions, and two effect sizes for the influence of context on the cognitive parameter, yielding 60 conditions. The proposed full grid would use 1,000 replications per condition, for 60,000 replications, after a small model-recovery pilot establishes feasibility and its compute budget is approved. We will analyze the number of false selections using Bayes factors and the leave-one-out information criterion, and the overall degree of absolute bias.

**Hypotheses**

We hypothesize that omitting contextual variables will result not only in biased parameter estimates, as documented previously, but also in incorrect model selection, such that established model-selection criteria will select categorically different cognitive models. Formal proofs will explore the effects of omitting contextual factors when their variance is not explained by the parameters from more complex models.
